## 1. Matching images and descriptions is a classification problem

A classifier normally chooses among a fixed list of labels. A contrastive model instead learns to compare an image representation with a text representation. If compatible inputs point in similar directions, a description can act as a class prototype or a retrieval query.

Start with a batch of $B$ matched image/text pairs $(x_i,t_i)$. For image $i$, the batch supplies one correct text index and $B-1$ alternatives. Predicting the correct index turns “bring matches together” into an explicit probability objective.

CLIP-style dual encoders encode the two modalities separately. They do not generate answers, and their global similarity score does not identify which image region supports a sentence.

## 2. Shared width does not mean shared encoder

Let image encoder $f_\theta$ and text encoder $g_\phi$ produce vectors of width $d$. Parameters $\theta,\phi$ need not be shared. Normalize their outputs:

$$
\mathbf{v}_i=\frac{f_\theta(x_i)}{\lVert f_\theta(x_i)\rVert_2},
\qquad
\mathbf{u}_i=\frac{g_\phi(t_i)}{\lVert g_\phi(t_i)\rVert_2}.
$$

These ideal equations assume nonzero vectors; implementations use an epsilon to handle tiny norms. Both $\mathbf{v}_i,\mathbf{u}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. Stack them into $\mathbf{V},\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d}$.

The dot product of unit vectors is cosine similarity. To convert similarity into a useful logit scale, choose temperature $\tau>0$:

$$
S_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_j}{\tau},
\qquad
\mathbf{S}=\frac{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf T}}{\tau}.
$$

The score matrix has shape $(B,B)$, not $(d,d)$. Lower temperature sharpens differences. CLIP learns a logit-scale parameter in its full training setup; our lab fixes $\tau=0.2$ so the geometry and loss can be studied separately.

## 3. Derive the objective one anchor at a time

Fix image $i$. Its probability of selecting text $j$ is a row-wise softmax:

$$
p(j\mid x_i)=\frac{\exp S_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^{B}\exp S_{ik}}.
$$

The paired description is $j=i$. Maximizing its probability means minimizing its negative log-probability:

$$
\ell_i^{I\to T}
=-S_{ii}+\log\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp S_{ij}.
$$

Average over images. Then reverse the task: for text $j$, classify the corresponding image using a column-wise softmax. The column loss is $\ell_j^{T\to I}=-S_{jj}+\log\sum_i\exp S_{ij}$. The symmetric objective is

$$
L=\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\ell_i^{I\to T}
+\frac{1}{2B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\ell_j^{T\to I}.
$$

This is an InfoNCE-style contrastive objective. It encourages discrimination among the sampled alternatives; its behavior depends on how those alternatives are sampled. “Mutual-information bound” interpretations require additional assumptions and are not needed to implement this classifier.

中文确认：正样本不是“概率应该等于某个余弦值”，而是正确配对的索引。负样本参与 softmax 的分母，因此也会产生梯度。

## 4. Why the gradient pulls and pushes

For a row loss, use the derivative of log-sum-exp:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_i^{I\to T}}{\partial S_{ij}}
=p(j\mid x_i)-\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}.
$$

For the matching text, this is negative unless probability is already one; a descent step tends to raise that logit. For other texts, it is positive; a descent step tends to lower them.

Let $P^{r}_{ij}$ be row-softmax and $P^{c}_{ij}$ be column-softmax. Combining both directions gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial S_{ij}}
=\frac{P^{r}_{ij}+P^{c}_{ij}-2\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}}{2B}.
$$

Because each score uses a dot product, the gradient then travels into both encoders. Normalization adds a Jacobian: if $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{a}/r$, $r=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert_2>0$, then

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial\mathbf{a}}
=\frac{1}{r}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T}\right).
$$

This follows from differentiating the numerator and the norm in the denominator. The subtraction removes the component parallel to $\mathbf{v}$: the normalized representation primarily changes direction rather than magnitude.

### Complete the chain to the unnormalized encoder outputs

Let $\mathbf{G}_S=\nabla_{\mathbf{S}}L$. From $S_{ij}=\sum_kV_{ik}U_{jk}/\tau$, summing all paths through a feature gives

$$
\mathbf{G}_V=\frac{\mathbf{G}_S\mathbf{U}}{\tau},
\qquad
\mathbf{G}_U=\frac{\mathbf{G}_S^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{V}}{\tau}.
$$

Both have shape $(B,d)$. To derive normalization's Jacobian coordinate by coordinate, write $v_j=a_j/r$ and $\partial r/\partial a_k=a_k/r$. The quotient rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial a_k}
=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{r}-\frac{a_ja_k}{r^3}
=\frac{\delta_{jk}-v_jv_k}{r}.
$$

Here $\delta_{jk}$ is 1 when $j=k$ and 0 otherwise. Therefore, for upstream $\mathbf{g}_v$,

$$
\mathbf{g}_a=
\frac{\mathbf{g}_v-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{g}_v)}{r}.
$$

Its dot product with $\mathbf{v}$ is zero: this local gradient lies in the tangent plane of the unit sphere. This exact formula assumes $r$ exceeds the normalization epsilon; the clamped tiny-norm branch behaves differently.

The companion now tests these complete gradients on **non-symmetric** scores. A symmetric two-pair fixture alone can conceal accidentally swapping row and column operations.

## 5. A two-pair numerical example

Let $\mathbf{S}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$. Each correct probability is $e^2/(e^2+1)\approx0.8808$. Each row loss is approximately $0.1269$, and the symmetric mean is also $0.1269$.

The diagonal score gradient is $(0.8808-1)/2\approx-0.0596$; an off-diagonal gradient is $0.1192/2\approx0.0596$. The division by two comes from batch averaging after the two identical directions are combined.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
scores = torch.tensor([[2., 0.], [0., 2.]], requires_grad=True)
labels = torch.arange(2)
loss = (F.cross_entropy(scores, labels) +
        F.cross_entropy(scores.T, labels)) / 2
loss.backward()
expected = ((scores.detach().softmax(1) - torch.eye(2)) +
            (scores.detach().softmax(0) - torch.eye(2))) / 4
torch.testing.assert_close(scores.grad, expected)
print(float(loss.detach()), scores.grad)
```

## 6. False negatives and several valid matches

Two images may both be correctly described as “a horizontal bar.” Treating one caption as correct and its duplicate as wrong creates an impossible distinction if the captions have identical embeddings.

Let $\mathcal{P}_i$ be all acceptable text indices for image $i$. One multi-positive objective maximizes total probability on this set:

$$
\ell_i=-\log
\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_i}\exp S_{ij}}
{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp S_{ij}}.
$$

This is a difference of two log-sum-exp terms. It differs from averaging the negative log-probability of every positive individually: the total-mass objective can put most mass on one easy positive. Choose based on the task.

If all four captions are equivalent and all logits equal zero, diagonal CE is $\log4$ but the total-positive-mass loss is zero. Zero is appropriate only for this “any equivalent caption counts” objective; it does not mean the embedding geometry is universally useful.

### Multi-positive gradients explain the objective's behavior

Define $P_{ij}$ as the row softmax over every candidate and $Q_{ij}$ as a softmax restricted to the positive set (zero outside it). Differentiating the two log-sum-exp terms gives, before batch averaging,

$$
\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial S_{ij}}=P_{ij}-Q_{ij}.
$$

For logits $(2,1,0)$ and positives at indices 1 and 2 in mathematical one-based numbering, $P\approx(.6652,.2447,.0900)$ and $Q\approx(.7311,.2689,0)$. The gradient is approximately $(-.0658,-.0242,.0900)$. The higher-scoring positive receives the larger attraction in this example. The lab checks this derivative and rejects an empty positive set.

With batch size one and one matched pair, ordinary contrastive loss is zero for every score and gives no discrimination gradient. More training steps cannot repair the absence of alternatives in that objective.

## 7. Retrieval and zero-shot classification

At inference, encode candidate class descriptions $t_c$ once and choose

$$
\widehat c=\arg\max_c\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_c.
$$

A common full-model use is zero-shot transfer: no supervised training on the target classification dataset, although the broad pretraining corpus may already contain related concepts. Prompt wording, domain shift, candidate labels, and pretraining overlap matter.

Our lab learns two text ID vectors and an image MLP on synthetic bars. It validates matching, gradients, and classification of new images. It is **not** a natural-language text encoder and **not** evidence of unseen-class zero-shot transfer.

## 8. Experimental pitfalls

The companion compares single-positive and multi-positive fixtures, trains the grouped objective, and reports independent-image accuracy. No test labels are used in training.

Do not interpret a softmax over candidate descriptions as a calibrated probability that a sentence is true. Adding an irrelevant class changes the normalization. A closed candidate list always yields an argmax even when no description fits.

Large batches provide more alternatives but also more false negatives and memory cost. Distributed contrastive training additionally needs correct feature gathering and gradient semantics; our local experiment does not implement that.
