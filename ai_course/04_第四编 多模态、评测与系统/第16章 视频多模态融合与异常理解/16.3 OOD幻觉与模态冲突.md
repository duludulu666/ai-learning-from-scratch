## 1. Separate three different failure questions

**Distribution shift:** are deployment inputs distributed differently from training or validation inputs?

**Hallucination:** does a generated claim lack support in the provided evidence or contradict it?

**Modality conflict:** do different sources support incompatible conclusions?

These overlap, but they are not synonyms. A model can hallucinate on an ordinary in-distribution image. Two correctly functioning sensors can disagree because they describe different timestamps. An OOD input may still be answered correctly.

We need separate tests and clear abstention behavior, rather than assuming a single confidence number solves every problem.

## 2. Why a softmax confidence can be misleading

For logits $z_1,\ldots,z_C$, softmax returns

$$
p_c=\frac{\exp z_c}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp z_j}.
$$

This normalizes relative scores among the supplied classes. There is no “none of the above” term unless explicitly modeled. Large relative logits can produce high confidence even far from the training data.

Construct a two-dimensional input $\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2)$ and classifier logits $(-5x_1,5x_1)$. Known data are near $(-1,0)$ and $(1,0)$. Move either point to $x_2=20$. The classifier ignores $x_2$, so confidence is unchanged, about 0.99995, even though the input is far from those training clusters.

中文确认：softmax 只是在已有选项之间分配相对概率，不会自动判断“这是不是我见过的世界”。

The laboratory makes this counterexample exact. A nearest-prototype distance detects this specially constructed shift, but could miss a semantic shift that preserves distance. No universal detector is established.

## 3. OOD detection is another scored prediction task

Define $o_i=1$ for an OOD example and $o_i=0$ for ID. Choose a scalar score $s_i$ that increases with suspected OOD-ness, such as $1-\max_c p_c$ or an embedding distance.

A threshold $\tau$ gives

$$
\widehat o_i=\mathbf{1}_{\{s_i\geq\tau\}}.
$$

Choose $\tau$ on a suitable validation design, not the final OOD test set. Report which shift families were available during development. Tuning on one known shift does not establish performance on all unknown shifts.

AUROC measures ranking across ID/OOD pairs; 17.1 derives it. Also report an operating point, false alarms on ID, missed OOD inputs, and downstream task quality. A detector that rejects everything has no deployment usefulness.

## 4. Selective prediction: trade coverage for error

Let $a_i(\tau)\in\{0,1\}$ mean the system accepts example $i$ at threshold $\tau$, and $e_i=1$ indicate an incorrect accepted prediction. For $n$ total examples, define

$$
\operatorname{coverage}(\tau)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i(\tau).
$$

Among accepted examples, selective risk is

$$
R(\tau)=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i(\tau)e_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i(\tau)}.
$$

If nothing is accepted, risk is undefined, not zero. Otherwise, a lower risk can result simply from answering fewer cases; report both quantities.

For five predictions with confidence $(.99,.90,.85,.70,.55)$ and correctness $(1,1,0,1,0)$, accepting all gives coverage 1 and risk $2/5$. At threshold .8, coverage is $3/5$ and risk $1/3$. At .95, coverage is $1/5$ and risk zero on this small sample. None of these empirical numbers proves future safety.

### A higher threshold does not guarantee lower risk

Coverage decreases as the acceptance threshold rises, but empirical risk need not decrease. If two cases have confidence $(.99,.80)$ and correctness $(0,1)$, accepting both gives risk .5, while accepting only the more confident case gives risk 1.

The original five-case example illustrates one favorable ordering, not a monotonicity theorem. The extended companion tests this counterexample and the no-accepted-example result $(\text{coverage}=0,\text{risk}=\mathrm{None})$.

OOD distance scores need not lie in $[0,1]$. AUROC only compares ordering, so the helper now accepts arbitrary finite real scores. Calibration and Brier still require probabilities; do not normalize distances merely to satisfy an unnecessarily restrictive API.

## 5. Contradiction is evidence to inspect, not erase

Suppose visual probabilities are $(.95,.05)$ and text-source probabilities are $(.05,.95)$. Their equal mixture is $(.5,.5)$. That uncertainty hides the reason: both streams were individually confident but disagreed.

Keep source-specific outputs, timestamps, provenance, and missingness. Ask whether one source is stale, one modality contains instructions rather than observations, the label definition differs, or the model lacks an appropriate category.

An uncertainty policy might request another observation, return a bounded statement of conflict, or route to review. It should not invent a confident narrative that makes the streams appear consistent.

## 6. Grounding a generated claim

A response can be decomposed into claims. For each claim, identify whether supplied evidence supports, contradicts, or fails to determine it. “No evidence of an object” differs from “evidence that no object exists,” especially under occlusion or low resolution.

A useful fixture might contain:

```text
Observation: a small red shape is partly hidden.
Question: what exact object is it?
Supported answer: the visible region is red; exact identity is uncertain.
Unsupported answer: it is definitely a particular model of vehicle.
```

This is an annotation example, not an actual VLM output. The lab uses only numeric fixtures; it does not evaluate real-model hallucination rates.

For a real model, collect image/question/response triples, use a claim-level rubric, calibrate annotator agreement, and test image swaps plus unanswerable questions. An LLM judge is itself a fallible measurement instrument, especially if it cannot inspect the evidence.

### Standalone no-coverage check

```python
import torch
correct = torch.tensor([1, 0])
confidence = torch.tensor([.9, .8])
accepted = confidence >= 1.0
coverage = accepted.float().mean().item()
risk = (1 - correct[accepted].float()).mean().item() if accepted.any() else None
assert coverage == 0 and risk is None
```
