> Prerequisites: 02.4 and 04.1. Goal: derive how regularization changes training and test whether it helps held-out performance.

## 1. Motivation: many solutions fit the same training set

A sufficiently flexible model can fit useful structure and accidental details. Training loss alone does not specify which of several good fits should be preferred.

Regularization introduces additional preferences: smaller weights, predictions stable under valid input changes, or less dependence on individual hidden activations. These preferences help only when they suit the problem. Validation measures the tradeoff.

## 2. L2 regularization adds a cost for large weights

Let $L_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{w})$ be the data loss and $\lambda\geq0$ control the penalty. Using a factor of $1/2$ gives

$$
L_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathbf{w})
=L_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{w})
+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j w_j^2.
$$

Differentiate one term: $\partial(\lambda w_j^2/2)/\partial w_j=\lambda w_j$. Consequently, if $\mathbf{g}$ is the data gradient,

$$
\nabla L_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathbf{g}+\lambda\mathbf{w}.
$$

For plain SGD with learning rate $\eta$ and no momentum,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}
&=\mathbf{w}_t-\eta(\mathbf{g}_t+\lambda\mathbf{w}_t)\cr
&=(1-\eta\lambda)\mathbf{w}_t-\eta\mathbf{g}_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term shrinks the old weights. This explains the term **weight decay**. With $w=2$, $\eta=0.1$, $\lambda=0.05$, and no data gradient, the next weight is $1.99$.

For matrices use $\|\mathbf{W}\|_F^2$. Which parameters to penalize is a modeling choice; biases and normalization parameters are often excluded. Document the choice.

## 3. Why L2 and AdamW are different

The equivalence above is specific to plain SGD with the stated conventions. Adam transforms gradients using moving averages and coordinate-wise scaling. If $\lambda\mathbf{w}$ is inserted into the gradient, it also enters these averages and is adaptively scaled.

AdamW instead uses the data gradient for Adam's moment estimates and applies a separate shrinkage:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{t+1}
=(1-\eta_t\lambda)\mathbf{w}_t
-\eta_t
\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_t}
{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\varepsilon}.
$$

Here $\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_t$ and $\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t$ are the bias-corrected moments from 04.1; all division is coordinate-wise.

For a concrete first-step contrast, take $w=2$, data gradient $g=0.4$, $\eta=0.1$, and $\lambda=0.05$, ignoring tiny $\varepsilon$. Adding L2 makes Adam's effective gradient $0.4+0.05(2)=0.5$; its first normalized step is $-0.1$, giving $w'=1.9$. AdamW separately shrinks $2$ to $1.99$ and subtracts $0.1$, giving $1.89$. The formulas already differ at the first step; later their moment histories can differ too.

The companion script compares explicit-L2 Adam against coupled-decay Adam over several steps and separately verifies decoupled AdamW.

This decouples shrinkage from Adam's gradient normalization. It does not make the effect independent of learning rate or number of steps: repeated factors $(1-\eta_t\lambda)$ accumulate over training.

中文提示：给 loss 加 L2 项与 AdamW 的独立衰减一般不是同一个算法。比较实验时，必须说明你实际用了哪一种。

## 4. Dropout: derive the scaling from the expectation

Suppose a hidden activation $a_j$ should sometimes be unavailable during training. Let dropout probability be $p$ and keep probability $q=1-p$, with $0<q\leq1$. Draw a mask $m_j\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)$.

If we only used $m_ja_j$, the average activation would shrink to $qa_j$. To preserve its expectation, use inverted dropout:

$$
\tilde a_j=\frac{m_j a_j}{q},
\qquad
\mathbb E[\tilde a_j]=\frac{\mathbb E[m_j]a_j}{q}=a_j.
$$

For $a_j=2$ and dropout probability $p=0.5$, a training activation is 0 or 4 with equal probability, giving mean 2. Its variance is 4, not zero. More generally, since $m_j^2=m_j$,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde a_j)
=\frac{a_j^2}{q}-a_j^2=\frac{p}{q}a_j^2.
$$

Preserving the mean does not mean preserving each forward value.

At evaluation time, use $a_j$ directly. This is PyTorch's standard convention.

The full network's expected prediction is not generally unchanged because later layers contain nonlinear operations: in general $\mathbb E[f(\tilde{\mathbf{a}})]\neq f(\mathbb E[\tilde{\mathbf{a}}])$. Dropout is a stochastic training modification, not an exact averaging theorem for whole models.

## 5. Data augmentation encodes an assumption about labels

If a transformation $T$ should preserve the label, training can minimize an average over transformed inputs:

$$
L_{\mathrm{aug}}
=\mathbb E_{(\mathbf{x},y)}
 \mathbb E_T[\ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(T(\mathbf{x})),y)].
$$

For example, a small translation may preserve an object category. Rotation may change the label of an orientation task or a handwritten digit. An invalid augmentation teaches the wrong invariance.

Apply random augmentation during training; keep validation preprocessing deterministic for repeatable comparison. Fit normalization using training data.

## 6. Early stopping selects how much fitting to allow

As training proceeds, validation may stop improving before training error stops falling. Save the best validation checkpoint and stop after a chosen patience interval without sufficient improvement.

The stopping rule is a hyperparameter. Do not repeatedly change it after observing test results. Restore the best weights rather than automatically keeping the last epoch.

For Adam, resuming a checkpoint also requires optimizer state; the moving averages affect the next update.

## 7. Runnable experiment: three mechanisms checked numerically

```python
import torch

torch.manual_seed(4)
torch.set_num_threads(1)

# L2-in-the-loss equals decay for one plain-SGD step.
w1 = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([2., -3.], dtype=torch.float64))
w2 = torch.nn.Parameter(w1.detach().clone())
g = torch.tensor([0.4, -0.2], dtype=torch.float64)
lr, decay = 0.1, 0.05
opt1 = torch.optim.SGD([w1], lr=lr, weight_decay=0)
opt2 = torch.optim.SGD([w2], lr=lr, weight_decay=decay)
loss1 = (w1 * g).sum() + 0.5 * decay * w1.square().sum()
loss1.backward()
(w2 * g).sum().backward()
opt1.step()
opt2.step()
torch.testing.assert_close(w1, w2)

# AdamW: verify the first step with known data gradients.
w3 = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor([2., -3.], dtype=torch.float64))
old = w3.detach().clone()
adamw = torch.optim.AdamW([w3], lr=lr, weight_decay=decay, eps=1e-8)
(w3 * g).sum().backward()
adamw.step()
expected = old * (1 - lr * decay) - lr * g / (g.abs() + 1e-8)
torch.testing.assert_close(w3, expected)

# Dropout preserves activation expectation, approximately over many samples.
dropout = torch.nn.Dropout(p=0.5)
values = torch.full((20000,), 2.0)
dropout.train()
print("training mean:", dropout(values).mean().item())
dropout.eval()
torch.testing.assert_close(dropout(values), values)
print("plain SGD result:", w1.detach())
print("AdamW first step:", w3.detach())
```

The checks validate the formulas; they do not prove improved generalization. That needs held-out experiments.

## 8. Runnable generalization experiment: noisy, underdetermined regression

Use more features than training examples so many solutions can fit. Only a few features generate the target. Compare ridge penalties using the same data.

Why does appending rows implement ridge? Let $\mathbf{A}:(N,D)$ be the training design and $\mathbf{a}:(N,)$ its targets. Stack

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}\cr\sqrt{N\lambda}\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}\cr\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The squared residual norm becomes $\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a}\|_2^2+N\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$. Divide by $2N$ to recover the stated half-MSE plus penalty. Omitting $\sqrt{N}$ would implement a different effective regularization strength. For $\lambda>0$, the optimum also satisfies

$$
\frac{\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{a})}{N}
+\lambda\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0},
$$

which the companion script checks independently.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
X = rng.normal(size=(180, 40))
truth = np.zeros(40)
truth[:3] = [2., -1., 0.5]
y = X @ truth + rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=180)
A, V = X[:25], X[25:]
a, v = y[:25], y[25:]

# Objective: mean squared error / 2 + lambda * ||w||^2 / 2.
# Augmenting the system gives the same ridge objective.
for lam in [0.0, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0]:
    augmented_X = np.vstack([A, np.sqrt(len(A) * lam) * np.eye(40)])
    augmented_y = np.r_[a, np.zeros(40)]
    weights, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(augmented_X, augmented_y, rcond=None)
    train_mse = np.mean((A @ weights - a)**2)
    val_mse = np.mean((V @ weights - v)**2)
    print(lam, train_mse, val_mse, np.linalg.norm(weights))
```

Moderate regularization can trade higher training error for lower validation error; excessive regularization can underfit. Report what this seed shows and repeat before drawing a broad conclusion.

## 9. Fair comparison and common mistakes

Keep data, initial parameters, batch order, and update budget controlled. Compare validation **data loss**, not a regularized training loss against an unregularized validation loss. Tune learning rates fairly when comparing optimizers.

Do not add a penalty to the loss and also enable optimizer decay unless the combination is intentional. Do not assume dropout always helps a small model, or that every spatial augmentation preserves every label.
