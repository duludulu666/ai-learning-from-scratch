## 0. How to use this lesson

Do not try to memorize all formulas on the first pass. Follow the order:

1. Understand what training is trying to do.
2. Use a one-dimensional example to see why the update direction matters.
3. Derive gradient descent from a local approximation.
4. See why the learning rate can help or destroy training.
5. Understand mini-batch SGD as an efficient approximation.
6. Add momentum to solve oscillation and slow movement.
7. Add Adam's scale normalization and bias correction.
8. Verify the ideas with a controlled PyTorch experiment.

The formulas are consequences of these questions. They are not arbitrary rules.

## 1. What is an optimizer trying to do?

Suppose a model has parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and a loss function $L(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. The model has already produced a prediction, and backpropagation has computed how the loss changes with respect to every parameter.

The optimizer must now answer:

> How should the parameters change so that the next loss is likely to be smaller?

For one parameter, imagine the loss curve below conceptually:

```text
loss
  ^             • current point
  |           /
  |          /
  |_________/____________> parameter
```

At the current point, the slope tells us what happens if the parameter increases slightly:

- positive slope: increasing the parameter increases the loss, so move left;
- negative slope: increasing the parameter decreases the loss, so move right;
- zero slope: the first-order information gives no preferred direction.

This slope is the one-dimensional gradient.

## 2. The simplest possible case: one scalar parameter

Let the parameter be $\theta$ and the loss be:

$$
L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-3)^2.
$$

The minimum is at $\theta=3$. Different starting values give different slopes:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}\theta}=\theta-3.
$$

### Start at $\theta=0$

The gradient is $-3$. A negative slope means the loss decreases when we move to the right. Therefore the update should increase $\theta$.

### Start at $\theta=5$

The gradient is $2$. A positive slope means the loss decreases when we move to the left. Therefore the update should decrease $\theta$.

In both cases, the useful direction is opposite to the gradient. This motivates “subtract the gradient”, but we still need a mathematical explanation and a step size.

## 3. Why does subtracting the gradient help?

For a small change $\Delta\theta$, Taylor's theorem gives the local approximation:

$$
L(\theta+\Delta\theta)
\approx
L(\theta)+
\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\Delta\theta.
$$

Let:

$$
g=\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}\theta}.
$$

The change in loss is approximately:

$$
\Delta L\approx g\,\Delta\theta.
$$

We want this product to be negative. The choice that always has the correct sign is:

$$
\Delta\theta=-\eta g,
$$

where $\eta>0$ is a small positive number called the learning rate.

Substituting this choice:

$$
\Delta L
\approx
g(-\eta g)
=-\eta g^2.
$$

Since $\eta>0$ and $g^2\ge0$, this first-order prediction is non-positive. Therefore the update is:

$$
\boxed{\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}\theta}}.
$$

The minus sign is not a convention to memorize. It is the result of choosing the direction that makes the first-order change negative.

## 4. Why is the learning rate necessary?

The gradient gives a direction and a local sensitivity, but it does not tell us how far to move. The learning rate controls the distance.

For $L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-3)^2$ and $\theta_0=0$:

$$
\theta_1=0-\eta(-3)=3\eta.
$$

### Case A: $\eta=0.1$

$$
\theta_1=0.3.
$$

The parameter moves toward $3$, but slowly.

### Case B: $\eta=1$

$$
\theta_1=3.
$$

For this particular quadratic, one step reaches the minimum.

### Case C: $\eta=2$

$$
\theta_1=6.
$$

The direction was correct, but the step overshot the minimum. Both the old and new losses equal $4.5$; the next update returns to zero. This is oscillation with unchanged loss, not loss growth.

### Case D: $\eta=2.2$

The new parameter is $6.6$, so the loss is $(6.6-3)^2/2=6.48$, larger than $4.5$. In this example, a rate above 2 causes the error magnitude to grow. Therefore:

> A correct gradient direction does not guarantee a lower loss when the step is too large.

## 5. The multi-dimensional version

Real models have millions or billions of parameters. Let:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
\qquad
\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_1}\cr
\vdots\cr
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_d}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For a small vector change $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$:

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta})
\approx
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
+\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\mathsf T}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}.
$$

The first-order change is the dot product:

$$
\Delta L\approx
\nabla L^{\mathsf T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}.
$$

If we constrain the update to have a fixed length, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives:

$$
\nabla L^{\mathsf T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
\ge
-\lVert\nabla L\rVert_2
\lVert\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}\rVert_2.
$$

The lower bound is attained when the update points opposite to the gradient. Thus the local steepest-descent direction is $-\nabla L$.

The vector update becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t
-\eta\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)
}.
$$

In Python, this is conceptually (pseudocode; a real manual tensor update belongs inside `torch.no_grad()`):

```text
parameter -= learning_rate * parameter.grad
```

## 6. Why is the loss not guaranteed to decrease every step?

The first-order approximation ignores curvature. Including the second-order term gives:

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta})
\approx
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
+\nabla L^{\mathsf T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
+\frac{1}{2}
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathsf T}
H
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta},
$$

where $H$ is the Hessian matrix of second derivatives. If $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}=-\eta\nabla L$:

$$
\Delta L
\approx
-\eta\lVert\nabla L\rVert_2^2
+\frac{\eta^2}{2}
\nabla L^{\mathsf T}H\nabla L.
$$

The first term is negative and favors progress. The second term depends on curvature and grows quadratically with $\eta$. If $\eta$ is too large, the positive curvature term can dominate, causing overshooting or divergence.

For a positive-definite quadratic whose largest Hessian eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\max}$, the following is an exact sufficient step-size condition for contraction toward the minimum:

$$
0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}.
$$

Deep networks are non-convex and their curvature changes during training, so this condition is mainly conceptual. In practice, we use loss curves, gradient norms, and learning-rate experiments.

## 7. Full-batch gradient descent versus mini-batch SGD

Suppose the dataset has $n$ examples and each example contributes loss $\ell_i$:

$$
L(\boldsymbol{\theta})
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
$$

The exact gradient is:

$$
\nabla L
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla\ell_i.
$$

Computing this over the whole dataset at every update may be expensive. Choose a mini-batch $B$ and estimate:

$$
\mathbf{g}_t
=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}
\nabla\ell_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t).
$$

If indices are drawn uniformly at fixed parameters, each example is equally likely, so the expected mini-batch mean gradient equals the full-data gradient. For two per-example scalar gradients $-3$ and $+1$, the full mean is $-1$. A one-example draw can still give $+1$, pointing opposite to that full-data direction. This is why one stochastic update need not lower the full-data loss.

The unbiased statement depends on the sampling scheme and per-example objective. Nonuniform sampling needs suitable weighting, and operations coupling examples, such as training-mode BatchNorm, complicate a simple per-example decomposition.

This is called a stochastic or mini-batch gradient. It is usually cheaper, but it contains sampling noise:

- larger batches: more accurate, more memory and computation per update;
- smaller batches: noisier, cheaper updates and sometimes useful exploration.

PyTorch training uses this estimate (pseudocode with already-defined objects):

```text
optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
outputs = model(inputs)
loss = criterion(outputs, targets)
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
```

## 8. Momentum: why current gradients are not enough

Plain SGD uses only the current mini-batch gradient. Imagine a narrow valley: the gradient can point strongly left and right across the valley while making slow progress along the valley. This causes zig-zagging.

Momentum keeps a memory of previous gradients. Define a velocity:

$$
\mathbf{v}_t
=\beta\mathbf{v}_{t-1}+\mathbf{g}_t,
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_0=\mathbf{0}.
$$

Then update:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t-\eta\mathbf{v}_t.
$$

Here $\beta$ controls memory. If gradients keep pointing in the same direction, velocity accumulates. If they alternate, their contributions partially cancel. Momentum therefore smooths noisy directions and accelerates consistent directions.

For a constant scalar gradient $g$, expanding the recurrence gives $v_1=g$, $v_2=(1+\beta)g$, and $v_3=(1+\beta+\beta^2)g$. With alternating gradients, positive and negative contributions partially cancel. This expansion explains what “memory” means numerically.

For long runs with constant $g$ and $0\leq\beta\lt 1$, the sum approaches $g/(1-\beta)$. Thus adding momentum also changes the effective scale; keeping the same learning rate does not preserve identical step sizes.

Some implementations define $\mathbf{v}_t$ with an additional factor $(1-\beta)$; that is a different scaling convention, not a different core idea.

## 9. Adam: what problem is it solving?

Momentum smooths the direction, but it still uses the same global learning rate for every parameter coordinate. In a neural network, one coordinate may repeatedly receive gradients of magnitude 100 while another receives gradients of magnitude 0.001. A single global step size is not equally appropriate for both.

Adam adds coordinate-wise scale information.

### Step 1: track the average direction

Adam's first moment is an exponential moving average:

$$
\mathbf{m}_t
=\beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1}
+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t.
$$

This is a smoothed gradient direction, similar to momentum.

### Step 2: track squared magnitude

Adam also tracks squared gradients:

$$
\mathbf{v}_t
=\beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1}
+(1-\beta_2)
(\mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{g}_t).
$$

The square removes signs and estimates the scale of each coordinate's recent gradients.

### Step 3: correct the initial zero-history bias

Initially:

$$
\mathbf{m}_0=\mathbf{0},\qquad
\mathbf{v}_0=\mathbf{0}.
$$

At the first step, both moving averages are artificially close to zero because they have no history. Adam corrects this:

$$
\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_t
=\frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1-\beta_1^t},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t
=\frac{\mathbf{v}_t}{1-\beta_2^t}.
$$

### Why divide by $1-\beta^t$?

Expand an exponential moving average initialized at zero:

$$
\mathbf{m}_t=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_1^{t-k}\mathbf{g}_k.
$$

The weights add to $1-\beta_1^t$, not 1. Under a constant expected gradient $\mathbb E[\mathbf{g}_k]=\boldsymbol{\mu}$,

$$
\mathbb E[\mathbf{m}_t]
=(1-\beta_1^t)\boldsymbol{\mu}.
$$

Dividing by this missing weight sum removes the zero-initialization shrinkage under that assumption. The squared-gradient average has the same geometric-sum argument. The squared gradient estimates a second raw moment, not the variance unless the mean correction is also considered.

During actual training the gradient distribution changes. Bias correction addresses the zero initialization; it does not make the running estimates exact descriptions of the current distribution.

### Step 4: normalize the update coordinate-wise

The final update is:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
=\boldsymbol{\theta}_t
-\eta
\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_t}
{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t}+\varepsilon}.
$$

The roles are now clear:

- $\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_t$: smoothed direction;
- $\sqrt{\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t}$: estimated recent gradient magnitude;
- division: reduce steps on consistently large-gradient coordinates and enlarge relative steps on small-gradient coordinates;
- $\varepsilon$: numerical protection against division by zero;
- $\eta$: global scale of the final update.

The square root matters because $\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_t$ has squared-gradient units. Taking its square root produces an RMS scale comparable to the gradient numerator. This motivates normalization; it is not a proof that the resulting direction is always optimal or a true inverse-Hessian step.

### A complete first-step scalar example

Let $g_1=2$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, and ignore $\varepsilon$.

First moments:

$$
m_1=(1-0.9)\cdot2=0.2,
\qquad
v_1=(1-0.999)\cdot2^2=0.004.
$$

Bias correction:

$$
\widehat m_1=\frac{0.2}{1-0.9}=2,
\qquad
\widehat v_1=\frac{0.004}{1-0.999}=4.
$$

Thus the normalized update is:

$$
\Delta\theta_1
=-\eta\frac{2}{\sqrt{4}}
=-\eta.
$$

Without bias correction, the first step would use $0.2/\sqrt{0.004}$, which is a quantity distorted by the zero initialization rather than by the actual gradient history.

### A second step explains what the first step hides

Continue with $g_2=-1$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$ and the moments above:

$$
m_2=0.9(0.2)+0.1(-1)=0.08,
\qquad
v_2=0.999(0.004)+0.001(1)=0.004996.
$$

After correction, $\widehat m_2=0.08/(1-0.9^2)\approx0.42105$ and $\widehat v_2=0.004996/(1-0.999^2)\approx2.49925$. Ignoring $\varepsilon$, the update is approximately $-0.26634\eta$.

The current gradient is negative, but the smoothed numerator is still positive because of the earlier gradient. Adam can therefore move **against the current gradient's descent direction**. Memory helps smooth noisy changes, but can lag a genuine direction change. Bias correction removes zero-initialization shrinkage; it does not remove this history.

## 10. What Adam does not guarantee

Adam is not a magic “always better” optimizer:

- a learning rate that is too large can still cause divergence;
- its loss need not decrease at every step;
- training loss and validation loss can behave differently;
- adaptive scaling can produce different generalization behavior from SGD;
- optimizer choice must be evaluated on the task, data, and compute budget.

The correct mental model is:

```text
SGD       = current local direction
Momentum  = smoothed direction over time
Adam      = smoothed direction + coordinate-wise scale normalization
```

## 11. Initialization and signal scale

Even a good optimizer can struggle if activations or gradients become extremely large or small. Initialization chooses the initial scale of weights.

For a layer with fan-in $n_{\mathrm{in}}$ and fan-out $n_{\mathrm{out}}$:

Consider a pre-activation $z=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}w_jx_j$. With independent, zero-mean weights and approximately independent input contributions,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(z)\approx
n_{\mathrm{in}}\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathbb E[x^2].
$$

A larger number of summed inputs increases scale unless each weight becomes correspondingly smaller. This motivates a weight variance proportional to $1/n_{\mathrm{in}}$.

For a symmetric pre-activation distribution, ReLU removes the negative half, giving approximately $\mathbb E[\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2]=\mathbb E[z^2]/2$. Compensating for this half-factor motivates the factor 2 in Kaiming initialization. This reasoning concerns second moments and relies on initialization assumptions; it is not an exact statement that every trained layer has fixed variance.

### Xavier/Glorot

Forward scale suggests weight variance $1/n_{\mathrm{in}}$ for near-linear zero-centered activations; applying the same argument to backward sums suggests $1/n_{\mathrm{out}}$. These cannot both hold when widths differ. Using $2/(n_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{out}})$ is a compromise based on the average of those widths, not an identity valid for every activation.

Often used with approximately symmetric activations such as `tanh`:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(W)\approx
\frac{2}{n_{\mathrm{in}}+n_{\mathrm{out}}}.
$$

### He/Kaiming

Designed for ReLU-like activations:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(W)\approx
\frac{2}{n_{\mathrm{in}}}.
$$

The goal is to keep the scale of forward activations and backward gradients reasonably stable across layers.

## 12. Normalization and gradient clipping

To normalize a feature vector, first subtract its mean so it is centered, then divide by its standard deviation to control scale. Learnable scale and shift let the model choose a useful output distribution afterward. The statistics must be computed over specified axes; 05.2 compares LayerNorm and BatchNorm in detail.

LayerNorm normalizes the chosen feature dimensions. For $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $\mu$ is the feature mean, $\sigma^2$ is its variance, $\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ are learned scale and shift, and $\varepsilon>0$ prevents division by zero:

$$
\mu=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_j,
\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(z_j-\mu)^2,
$$

$$
\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{z})
=\boldsymbol{\gamma}\odot
\frac{\mathbf{z}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\varepsilon}}
+\boldsymbol{\beta}.
$$

For global clipping, concatenate all parameter gradients conceptually into $\mathbf{g}$. Choose a positive maximum norm $c$. If $\|\mathbf{g}\|_2\leq c$, retain it. Otherwise multiply by $c/\|\mathbf{g}\|_2$, preserving its direction while reducing its length to $c$.

These two cases combine as follows for nonzero $\mathbf{g}$; define the zero-gradient case to remain zero:

$$
\mathbf{g}\leftarrow
\mathbf{g}\cdot
\min\left(1,
\frac{c}{\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_2}\right).
$$

For gradient $(3,4)$ with norm 5 and threshold $c=2$, global norm clipping gives $(1.2,1.6)$. Elementwise clipping to $[-2,2]$ would instead give $(2,2)$ and change direction: these are different operations.

With Adam, clipping the raw gradient does not directly bound the final update by $\eta c$, because moment history, normalization, and weight decay also affect the step. The companion experiment verifies the global norm rule and checks Xavier/Kaiming sample variances under their stated initialization settings.

Clipping is a safety mechanism. It should not hide a broken loss, invalid data, or an absurd learning rate.

## 13. A controlled experiment

To compare optimizers, change one factor at a time. Keep fixed:

- model architecture;
- dataset and train/validation split;
- random seed;
- batch size;
- number of updates;
- preprocessing;
- evaluation metric.

Log:

- training loss;
- validation loss;
- learning rate;
- gradient norm;
- optimizer hyperparameters;
- wall-clock time.

### Runnable experiment: controlled optimizer comparison

Every run resets the initial parameters and the same batch order. No clipping or weight decay is enabled here, so the baseline comparison isolates update rules more clearly. The reported gradient norm is the mean of pre-update batch norms, not only the final batch.

```python
import copy
import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(17)
torch.set_num_threads(1)
X = torch.randn(240, 4)
y = (X[:, :1] * 2 - X[:, 1:2] + 0.5 * X[:, 2:3].square())
y += 0.1 * torch.randn_like(y)
initial_model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 16), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(16, 1))
initial = copy.deepcopy(initial_model.state_dict())
order_rng = torch.Generator().manual_seed(18)
orders = [torch.randperm(180, generator=order_rng) for _ in range(35)]
factories = {
    "SGD": lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.03),
    "Momentum": lambda p: torch.optim.SGD(p, lr=0.01, momentum=0.9),
    "Adam": lambda p: torch.optim.Adam(p, lr=0.01),
    "AdamW(no decay)": lambda p: torch.optim.AdamW(p, lr=0.01, weight_decay=0),
}
for name, factory in factories.items():
    model = copy.deepcopy(initial_model)
    model.load_state_dict(initial)
    optimizer = factory(model.parameters())
    norms = []
    for order in orders:
        model.train()
        for indices in order.split(30):
            optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
            loss = (model(X[indices]) - y[indices]).square().mean()
            loss.backward()
            norm = torch.sqrt(sum(p.grad.square().sum() for p in model.parameters()))
            norms.append(norm.item())
            optimizer.step()
    model.eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        val_loss = (model(X[180:]) - y[180:]).square().mean().item()
    print(name, "validation MSE:", val_loss, "mean gradient norm:", sum(norms) / len(norms))
```

These rates are teaching choices, not equally optimized hyperparameters. Tune each optimizer on validation with comparable budgets before asserting superiority. Adam and AdamW with decay disabled should agree closely here; 04.2 isolates their regularization behavior.

## 14. Training diagnosis

### Loss does not decrease

Check the loss connection, target encoding, gradient values, learning rate, input scale, and model mode.

### Loss decreases extremely slowly

The learning rate may be too small; the model may be poorly conditioned, underpowered, or badly initialized.

### Loss becomes `NaN` or `Inf`

Check exploding activations, exploding gradients, invalid logarithms, division by zero, excessive learning rate, and mixed-precision overflow.

### Training loss is low but validation loss is high

This usually indicates overfitting, data leakage, distribution mismatch, or insufficient data diversity. The optimizer alone may not solve it.
