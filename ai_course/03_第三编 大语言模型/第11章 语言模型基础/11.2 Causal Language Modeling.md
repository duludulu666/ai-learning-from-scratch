## 1. Predict a whole sequence by predicting its pieces

A sentence is not one of a small fixed set of labels. Its length and content vary. Instead of classifying complete sentences, predict the next token repeatedly.

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_T$ be the content tokens followed by EOS, and let $x_0=\mathrm{BOS}$. The probability chain rule decomposes the joint sequence probability:

$$
p_\theta(x_1,\ldots,x_T\mid x_0)
=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_t\mid x_0,\ldots,x_{t-1}).
$$

This factorization is a probability identity; a Transformer is one possible parameterization of its conditional distributions. It does not assume tokens are independent.

We maximize the probability of observed training sequences. Products of many small numbers underflow, so take logarithms. Negating changes maximization into minimization:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)
=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{0:t-1}).
$$

That is why a next-token loss adds across positions.

## 2. Turn one sequence into supervised pairs

For `[BOS, the, red, fox, EOS]`, the aligned arrays are:

| Output position | Input token | Allowed history | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | BOS | BOS | the |
| 1 | the | BOS, the | red |
| 2 | red | BOS, the, red | fox |
| 3 | fox | BOS, the, red, fox | EOS |

The output at position 2 is not asked to reconstruct input `red`. It predicts `fox`. This one-position shift is essential.

Teacher forcing supplies the true earlier tokens for every position. A causal mask ensures that output position 2 cannot read input position 3, which already contains its target. All output positions can therefore be computed in parallel during training without giving them future information.

During free generation, the model consumes its own previous samples. Errors can change later contexts. Good teacher-forced loss is relevant, but it does not fully characterize long free-running behavior.

## 3. From logits to loss and its gradient

At one output position, the model produces logits $\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^V$. Define

$$
p_k=\frac{\exp z_k}{\sum_{j=0}^{V-1}\exp z_j}.
$$

For target ID $y$, negative log probability becomes

$$
\ell=-\log p_y=-z_y+\log\sum_j\exp z_j.
$$

Differentiate the two terms separately. The first contributes $-1$ only at the target coordinate. The derivative of log-sum-exp contributes $p_k$:

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_k}=p_k-\mathbf1[k=y].
$$

For probabilities $[0.6,0.3,0.1]$ and target 1 under zero-based IDs, the gradient is $[0.6,-0.7,0.1]$. The loss is $-\log0.3$. This is a logit-space statement; updating shared neural-network parameters can affect many positions simultaneously.

### Connect the logit derivative to the vocabulary head

Flatten batch and time into $N=BT$ positions. Let hidden states be $\mathbf H\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}$, output weights $\mathbf W\in\mathbb R^{V\times d}$ and optional bias $\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^V$. Then $\mathbf Z=\mathbf H\mathbf W^{\mathsf T}+\mathbf b$.

Let $\mathbf G$ contain the per-position logit derivatives, with masked rows zero and a division by the total valid count $M$ for mean loss. Applying the linear-layer chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf W}=\mathbf G^{\mathsf T}\mathbf H,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf b}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf G_{i,:},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf H}=\mathbf G\mathbf W.
$$

Each row contributes an outer product to the shared output weight. If input/output embeddings are tied, the same parameter also collects the input-lookup path's gradient; the head contribution is not the entire gradient. Our TinyLM uses an untied, bias-free head. The companion test includes bias to verify the general formula.

For numerical stability, compute $\log\sum_j e^{z_j}=m+\log\sum_j e^{z_j-m}$, where $m=\max_jz_j$. Adding a common constant to every logit leaves probabilities and cross-entropy unchanged. The lab checks logits near 10,000 without exponentiating them directly.

## 4. Count exactly the positions you supervise

A rectangular batch uses padding. Let $m_{b,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether target position $(b,t)$ is supervised, and let $M=\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}>0$. Then

$$
L=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{b,t}m_{b,t}\ell_{b,t}.
$$

EOS is a genuine target in this lesson; PAD is not. BOS is the initial context, not a target. SFT in 13.1 changes the mask to omit prompt targets while still exposing prompt inputs.

For lengths 4 and 3 including BOS/EOS, shifted target counts are 3 and 2, not 4 and 3. The companion script constructs these exact arrays.

Mathematical masking is not always safe as numerical multiplication. A NaN loss multiplied by zero remains NaN, and an invalid target ID can fail before multiplication. The shared implementation indexes valid logits/labels **before** calling cross-entropy, and rejects an all-invalid batch explicitly. This protects this loss operation; it does not repair a model that already produced NaNs in upstream operations or propagated them into valid positions. Fix the source of numerical failure rather than treating masking as a universal NaN remedy.

## 5. Why mean-of-means can be wrong

Suppose one evaluation batch has total NLL 2 over 2 tokens and another has total NLL 18 over 6 tokens. Their means are 1 and 3.

Averaging those means gives 2, but the corpus token-average is

$$
L_{\mathrm{corpus}}=\frac{2+18}{2+6}=2.5.
$$

Accumulate total NLL and token count across batches, then divide once. Likewise, correctly weighted gradient accumulation must account for different valid-token counts; see 12.3.

This lesson chooses token-weighted loss. Equal weighting of whole examples is another possible objective, but it is different and must be stated explicitly.

## 6. Perplexity has a precise definition

For natural-log cross-entropy $L$, define perplexity as

$$
\mathrm{PPL}=\exp(L).
$$

Substitute the mean log loss:

$$
\mathrm{PPL}
=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{M}\frac{1}{p_i}\right)^{1/M},
$$

where $p_i$ is the predicted probability assigned to the actual target at valid position $i$. It is the geometric mean inverse target probability, not an arithmetic mean of inverse probabilities.

For target probabilities $[0.5,0.25,0.5]$, inverse probabilities multiply to 16, so PPL is $16^{1/3}\approx2.52$. A uniform vocabulary distribution over $V$ tokens has PPL $V$.

Compare PPL only with compatible tokenization, data, conditioning and masking. Splitting each word into more tokens changes the unit. PPL also does not directly measure factual accuracy, tool correctness or instruction following.

Changing the log base changes the inverse: if loss is measured in bits with $\log_2$, perplexity is $2^L$, not $e^L$. A byte-normalized negative log likelihood can sometimes aid cross-tokenizer comparisons, but the raw texts, boundaries and scoring protocol must still match. It does not automatically solve every comparability issue.

## 7. Small numerical verification

```python
import math
import torch

p = torch.tensor([0.5, 0.25, 0.5], dtype=torch.float64)
nll = -p.log().mean()
assert math.isclose(float(nll.exp()), 16 ** (1 / 3))
z = torch.tensor(1., 0., -1., dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
target = torch.tensor([1])
loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(z, target)
loss.backward()
expected = z.detach().softmax(-1)
expected[0, 1] -= 1
torch.testing.assert_close(z.grad, expected)
print("perplexity:", float(nll.exp()), "gradient:", z.grad)
```
