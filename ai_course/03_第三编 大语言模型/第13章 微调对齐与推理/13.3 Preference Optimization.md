> Prerequisites: likelihood, SFT, softmax derivatives. Goal: understand preference pairs, derive DPO in small steps, and distinguish offline preference fitting from broader reinforcement learning.

## 1. A good answer is not always uniquely specified

SFT provides one demonstrated answer. Preference data can instead say that response $y_w$ is preferred to $y_l$ for the same prompt $x$.

A pair does not explain every dimension of quality. Annotators may prefer correctness, style, brevity or agreement, and can disagree. Length and presentation can become shortcuts. Keep prompt/source groups isolated across evaluation splits and record the preference criteria.

We first model pairwise preferences, then connect them to policy updates.

## 2. From reward difference to a preference probability

Suppose a scalar reward $r(x,y)$ represents desirability. A Bradley–Terry model uses

$$
P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)
=\sigma(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)),
\qquad
\sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-u)}.
$$

Equal rewards give probability 0.5. A reward difference $\log3$ gives probability 0.75. Adding the same prompt-dependent constant to every response reward does not change preferences.

An explicit reward model can be trained with negative log preference likelihood. RLHF can then optimize a policy against that reward, often with a penalty for drifting from a reference policy. Reward-model errors can be exploited; a high learned reward is not automatically genuine task success.

## 3. Why a reference policy appears

Let $\pi(y\mid x)$ be the trainable response distribution and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)$ a fixed reference. Consider, for one prompt, the idealized objective

$$
\max_\pi
\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)r(x,y)
-\beta\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$

subject to $\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)=1$ and $\beta>0$. The second sum is KL divergence: it penalizes moving probability away from the reference.

Assume finite reward and positive reference probability on the candidate support. Introduce a normalization multiplier $\lambda$ and differentiate with respect to $\pi(y\mid x)$:

$$
r(x,y)-\beta\left(\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+1\right)+\lambda=0.
$$

Rearrange and absorb prompt-only constants into a normalizer:

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)
=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)}{Z(x)}.
$$

Here $Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)$. Taking logs gives

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}
+\beta\log Z(x).
$$

This is the algebra behind DPO's reward parameterization, not a claim that a finite neural network reaches the unrestricted optimum.

## 4. Cancel the intractable constant in a pair

For two responses to the **same** prompt, $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels. Replace $\pi^{*}$ by the parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ and define

$$
\Delta_\theta=
\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)\right]
-\left[\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)\right].
$$

The preference logit is $z=\beta\Delta_\theta$. Negative log likelihood becomes

$$
L_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\log\sigma(z)
=\mathrm{softplus}(-z).
$$

Here $\mathrm{softplus}(u)=\log(1+\exp u)$. Since
$-\log\sigma(z)=\log(1+\exp(-z))$, the identity is direct, not a new objective. A numerical implementation evaluates softplus stably rather than exponentiating a huge argument.

The stable softplus form avoids computing tiny probabilities and taking their log separately.

At initialization with $\pi_\theta=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, $\Delta_\theta=0$, so loss is $\log2$. The objective asks the trainable policy to improve the winner's relative odds over the loser's **compared with the reference**.

For a two-response example, let reference probabilities be $[0.5,0.5]$ and current winner/loser probabilities $[0.6,0.4]$. Then

$$
\Delta_\theta=\log(0.6/0.5)-\log(0.4/0.5)=\log1.5\approx0.4055.
$$

With $\beta=0.2$, the preference logit is about 0.0811, inferred preference probability about 0.5203, and loss about 0.6534, below $\log2\approx0.6931$. A modest improvement in policy odds therefore produces a modest preference improvement, not certainty.

## 5. Derive the update direction

Differentiate with respect to the margin:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Delta_\theta}
=-\beta\sigma(-\beta\Delta_\theta).
$$

This is negative. Gradient descent therefore tends to increase the margin. Expanding through the policy yields

$$
\nabla_\theta L
=-\beta\sigma(-\beta\Delta_\theta)
\left[
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)
-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)
\right].
$$

The reference is frozen, so it contributes no parameter gradient. Do not forget to disable its updates.

This direction improves a **relative** quantity. With shared parameters or other examples, it does not guarantee the absolute winner probability increases at every step for every prompt.

In the ideal KL objective, larger $\beta$ means stronger pressure to remain near the reference. In the DPO loss, it also rescales margins and gradients. “Smaller beta always means weaker learning” is therefore an unreliable tuning rule.

## 6. Sequence log probabilities are sums

For response $y_1,\ldots,y_R$,

$$
\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)
=\sum_{t=1}^{R}\log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{\lt t}).
$$

Use response-target masking and include the chosen EOS convention. Prompt targets and padding do not belong to the response likelihood.

Compute log-softmax and gather only on valid positions if ignored labels use a sentinel such as -100. Gathering all labels before masking can fail on that sentinel; multiplying an invalid result by zero is not a repair. The shared `response_log_probs` returns one **sum** per response, rejects empty responses and is checked against separately scored unpadded examples.

Dividing by response length changes the score to a mean log probability, not the same sequence likelihood used in this derivation. Length-normalized variants are possible, but their objectives must be named and justified. Summed scores can also interact with length preferences, so inspect response-length distributions.

## 7. What the local experiment does

The lab trains a categorical policy over three candidate responses for each of two prompts. This isolates the DPO objective without pretending to train a large chat model. It verifies the margin derivative using autograd and central finite differences, then observes preferred-response odds change.

A second fixture computes masked sums of token log probabilities with unequal response lengths and verifies ignored-position gradients. It distinguishes sequence sums from means.

The extended test connects these pieces to an actual `TinyLM`: identical policy/reference copies score a chosen and rejected continuation of the same prompt, yielding initial loss $\log2$. The reference is frozen, the policy's complete parameter gradient matches the chain-rule expression, and one sufficiently small SGD step increases the relative margin on that pair. This is a decoder **gradient check**, not a trained preference-generalization benchmark.

The tabular experiment starts from a uniform policy but a deliberately nonuniform reference, so its initial loss need not equal $\log2$. The separate identical-copy test is the one that verifies that initialization identity.

```python
import math
import torch
z = torch.tensor(0.,dtype=torch.float64,requires_grad=True)
loss = torch.nn.functional.softplus(-z)
loss.backward()
assert math.isclose(float(loss.detach()),math.log(2))
assert math.isclose(float(z.grad),-.5)
print("equal-policy loss and logit gradient:",float(loss.detach()),float(z.grad))
```

The tabular policy does not assess semantic preferences or generalization. Its role is to validate the math and implementation contract.

### Code bridge: response sums before the pairwise loss

The array order below is `[chosen, rejected]` for a single prompt. The reduction scores each response by a **sum**, not a mean. A real batch needs explicit pairing metadata so unrelated prompts are never paired accidentally.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(3)
logits = torch.randn(2, 3, 4, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
targets = torch.tensor([[1, 2, -100], [2, 1, 3]])
valid = targets.ne(-100)
selected_logp = logits[valid].log_softmax(-1)
token_logp = selected_logp.gather(-1, targets[valid].unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
rows = torch.arange(2)[:, None].expand_as(targets)[valid]
scores = torch.zeros(2, dtype=torch.float64).index_add(0, rows, token_logp)
# An identical, frozen reference is represented by detached scores here.
reference_scores = scores.detach().clone()
delta = (scores[0]-reference_scores[0]) - (scores[1]-reference_scores[1])
loss = F.softplus(-0.2*delta)
loss.backward()
torch.testing.assert_close(loss.detach(), torch.tensor(2.,dtype=torch.float64).log())
assert logits.grad[~valid].abs().sum() == 0
```

This snippet is a logit-level bridge. The companion script uses separate decoder/reference models and verifies their complete parameter gradient.

## 8. Bridge to online RL and verifiable rewards

DPO fits fixed preference pairs. Online RL can sample responses from the current policy, evaluate them and update from fresh feedback. A verifier might run unit tests or check a mathematical result, replacing some human comparisons with task-specific rewards.

For a sampled response $y$ and reward $R(y)$, the score-function identity motivates

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(y)]
=\mathbb E\!\left[R(y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\right].
$$

For a finite response set and a reward independent of $\theta$, expand the expectation and differentiate term by term:

$$
\nabla_\theta\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)R(y)
=\sum_yR(y)\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(y)
=\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)R(y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y).
$$

A prompt-dependent baseline $b(x)$ cancels because
$\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)=\nabla_\theta\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)=0$.
If the reward itself depends on $\theta$ and is not treated as fixed, an additional derivative term is needed. The exact identity also assumes sampling from the stated policy; top-p or temperature sampling changes that policy.

Subtracting a prompt-dependent baseline preserves this expectation and can reduce variance. PPO-style methods restrict updates using policy ratios and clipping; group-relative methods compare multiple sampled outcomes for the same prompt. These are follow-on topics, not implementations supplied by this DPO lab.

A verifier can be incomplete or exploited. Passing visible tests is not the same as general correctness; verbosity and reward shortcuts can distort optimization. Evaluate held-out tasks, reward hacking, uncertainty and failure modes.
